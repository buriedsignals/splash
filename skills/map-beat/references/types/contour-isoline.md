# Contour / isoline

> **Not backed by a shipped implementation.** The source engine (`map-native`) designed this type
> and never built it — `skills/map-native/src/map-types.ts` explicitly omits it from the
> discriminator with the comment "designed but never built, so it has no component." Everything
> below is drawn from established cartographic practice, not from a rendered, QA'd component. Read
> it as a starting doctrine, verify it against your own render, and expect to find things the source
> material didn't anticipate.

## What it's for

A contour (isoline) map answers "where does this continuous field cross a given value" — elevation,
temperature, air pressure, rainfall, travel-time-from-a-point — by drawing lines that connect every
point sharing the same value, the way topographic elevation lines do. It is the type for a field that
varies smoothly across space with no natural boundary, where the reader's real question is about the
SHAPE of the gradient (steep here, flat there, a ridge running this way) rather than a value attached
to a named region.

## When NOT to use it, and what to use instead

Don't reach for a contour map when the underlying data is already binned into named regions —
countries, districts, postcodes. That's a choropleth's job, and forcing region data through a
contour-generation step manufactures a smooth field out of what is actually a step function,
implying a precision and continuity the data never had. Don't reach for it either when the field is
sparse — a handful of weather stations, a few dozen sensor readings — because contour generation
needs enough sample density to interpolate a believable surface; too few points and the lines are
mostly the interpolation algorithm's opinion, not the data's shape. If the story's real point is a
single threshold crossing (where does this cross zero, where does the flood line sit) rather than the
whole gradient, a single highlighted isoline with a fill on one side of it usually communicates more
plainly than a full contour set. And if the readers' question is "how much, here" at a specific named
place rather than the shape of change across the whole field, a set of proportional symbols or a
choropleth on real regions answers that more directly than asking someone to interpolate between two
printed contour lines by eye.

## The one thing that goes wrong

Contour lines are generated by interpolating a surface from sample points, and that interpolation is
invisible in the final image — a reader sees confident, continuous lines with no indication of how
much genuine data sits underneath them versus how much is the algorithm's guess between sparse
samples. The failure mode is a contour map that reads as authoritative measurement everywhere it is
actually confident nowhere — dense near the sample points, pure extrapolation everywhere else, with
nothing on the page distinguishing the two. A second, more mechanical failure: an unlabelled interval.
A contour set's value only exists if the reader knows what one band of the ramp between two lines
represents — a fixed step (every 100m, every 2°C) has to be stated once, explicitly, or the lines are
decorative curves with no attached number.

## What the drawing needs

Lines are generated at a fixed, evenly-spaced interval through the value range — an even step (every
5 units), never an arbitrary one, so a reader who learns the interval once can read any gap on the map
without re-checking a legend. Every Nth line (typically every 5th) is usually drawn heavier and gets
an inline value label directly on the line itself, breaking the line briefly to seat the number rather
than floating it beside the path — the same discipline as a route map's crossed-territory label,
placed ON the thing it names. Fill between contour bands with a sequential ramp exactly like a
choropleth's, if the space between lines is filled at all — that fill is the same value channel as the
lines, doing the same job with less precision, and both must agree with each other and with a single
shared legend. Line density near the sample points can honestly be denser than density far from them;
if the source data justifies showing that confidence gradient, a lighter or dashed treatment for
poorly-sampled regions is more honest than uniform line weight across areas with wildly different
sample coverage.

## The accessibility trap

The interval label is the only thing that makes an isoline map's shape a number instead of a picture —
without it, a colour-vision-deficient reader loses the ramp fill entirely and a reader relying on the
lines alone has no way to know if adjacent bands are 1 unit or 100 units apart. Print the interval once,
prominently, not just on the legend swatch but stated in the caption or title ("contour interval: 5°C")
so it survives being screen-read or printed in monochrome, where the fill's sequential ramp — this
type's only other channel — disappears entirely and the labelled lines are all that's left.
