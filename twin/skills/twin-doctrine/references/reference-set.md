# Reference set

Real, examined treatments of a named argument structure, each reduced to a **transferable
information-design lesson** — never a styling description. This is what the reference loop
(`twin-storyboard`'s movement ④) shows a journalist: not "here is a chart that looks nice," but
"here is how a real newsroom sequenced this exact kind of evidence, and here is the rule you can
lift out of it and apply to your own data." Copying the rule, not the surface, is the whole
point — see `anti-patterns.md`'s closing entry.

**This set is published, standalone, evidence-led newsroom graphics — the same test
`chart-beat`'s static work is held to.** A person narrating over a slide is a different genre from
a graphic that has to carry its own argument with no presenter in the room; a static chart is the
harder, more honest case, because nothing off-canvas can rescue it. Every row was verified before
it shipped, not guessed at: the outlet, the date and the locator are things actually seen on the
actual page or the actual frame — a fetched article, an archived snapshot, or (for the two video
rows) a real caption/transcript track pulled from the platform itself, never approximated. Live
reference research (finding a new row for an argument structure not yet represented here) runs the
same way — see the real thing, quote or describe what is actually there, cite a locator that can
actually be checked, or do not ship the row.

The **Moment** column is a locator, not necessarily a timecode: a published static chart has no
timecode, so its locator is the thing that actually identifies where in the piece the graphic
sits — a chart's own title or caption, its element id, a dated URL. The two video rows carry a
real timecode instead, verified against the platform's own caption track.

| Reference | Moment | Transferable lesson |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| John Burn-Murdoch — Financial Times — [coronavirus death-toll trajectories, posted to X/Twitter](https://x.com/jburnmurdoch/status/1239276487062233089) (16 March 2020) | the death-toll trajectory chart | Italy and Spain are drawn in colour, every other country recedes to grey, and the line ends in a label instead of a legend — so the one comparison the piece is about (are we tracking Italy) reads in the same glance as the shape of the curve itself. Neutral-for-comparison, colour-for-the-subject is not a palette choice here, it is the whole argument. |
| The New York Times, The Upshot — [Extensive Data Shows Punishing Reach of Racism for Black Boys](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2018/03/19/upshot/race-class-white-and-black-men.html) (19 March 2018) | the chart annotated "Black men consistently earn less than white men, regardless of whether they're raised poor or rich." | The sentence that states the finding is set directly on the chart as its own text layer (a `g-chart-anno` element), not left for the surrounding prose to supply — a reader who saw only the graphic, with no paragraph around it, would still walk away with the claim. Annotation carries the argument; the chart is not waiting on the article to explain itself. |
| Reuters Graphics — [Behind the Battleground States](https://www.reuters.com/graphics/USA-ELECTION/SWING-STATES/myvmadqlzvr/) (24 September 2024) | the "g-margin-box" chart, captioned by the line "the winning margins in those states in 2020 amounted to only about a quarter of one percent of votes" | A single, sourced number (a quarter of one percent) does the work an entire paragraph of hedged prose would otherwise have to do — the chart's job is to make one comparison (this year's margin against a visibly narrow band of history) impossible to misread, not to display every margin ever recorded. |
| Harry Stevens — The Washington Post — ["These simulations show how to flatten the coronavirus growth curve"](https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://www.washingtonpost.com/graphics/2020/world/corona-simulator/) (14 March 2020) | the four simulation panels run side by side under one shared clock | Every panel starts from the same population and the same single infection and runs on the same timeline — the only variable let loose between them is the behaviour rule. Because the data's own arrival (dots colliding, case counts climbing) is the event the reader watches happen, the argument for social distancing is demonstrated, not asserted; nothing needed to be said in words that the four clocks running side by side didn't already show. |
| The New York Times Visual Investigations — [How the Police Killed Breonna Taylor](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lDaNU7yDnsc) (2020) | 4:20 | Every account is anchored inside one fixed spatial reconstruction before it is heard — "in this reconstruction, we hear the official testimonies given by the two officers nearest the door." Competing, contradictory testimony accumulates inside a shared frame instead of asking the reader to hold each version separately in their head. |
| Vox — [The conflict in Kashmir, explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cyayif_nla8) (2019) | 3:51 | The map's defining boundary is drawn on screen at the exact moment the narration reaches its creation — "the UN Security Council brokered a ceasefire in 1949, which established this line." Synchronizing a map's geography with the story's chronology reads as cause and effect, where showing the finished border up front and explaining it after would read as trivia. |
| Alan Smith (UK Office for National Statistics) — [Why you should love statistics](https://www.ted.com/talks/alan_smith_why_you_should_love_statistics) (TED, 2016) — **a stage slide, not a published graphic; kept only because the technique below is not otherwise represented in this set** | 8:12 | Encoding a proportion as a count of repeating icons (Otto Neurath's isotype method), with the number sitting in the background rather than up front, avoids the words "percentage," "fraction" and "ratio" entirely — "a great way of representing quantity without resorting to using terms like percentage." Choosing the unit of encoding is an inclusion decision, not a decorative one — but note that here the case for it is made in the presenter's own voice, which is exactly the dependency a published graphic cannot have. |
