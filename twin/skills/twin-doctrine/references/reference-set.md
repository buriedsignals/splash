# Reference set

Real, watched treatments of a named argument structure, each reduced to a **transferable
information-design lesson** — never a styling description. This is what the reference loop
(`twin-storyboard`'s movement ④) shows a journalist: not "here is a chart that looks nice," but
"here is how a real newsroom or explainer sequenced this exact kind of evidence, and here is the
rule you can lift out of it and apply to your own data." Copying the rule, not the surface, is the
whole point — see `anti-patterns.md`'s closing entry.

Every row was verified against the actual video's own captions before it shipped: the quoted
moment is what is actually said on screen at that timecode, not a guess at where it "should" be.
Live reference research (finding a new row for an argument structure not yet represented here)
runs the same way — watch or read the real thing, quote what is actually there, timecode it
honestly, or do not ship the row.

| Reference | Moment | Transferable lesson |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Hans Rosling — [The best stats you've ever seen](https://www.ted.com/talks/hans_rosling_shows_the_best_stats_you_ve_ever_seen) (TED, 2006) | 7:46 | The chart spends a full static beat teaching its own encoding — "every bubble here is a country," size is population, the axes are named — before the animation is allowed to move ("if I now let the world move forward"). Legibility is earned before motion carries the argument; a reader who has not decoded the marks yet cannot follow a trajectory through them. |
| Alan Smith — [Why you should love statistics](https://www.ted.com/talks/alan_smith_why_you_should_love_statistics) (TED, 2016) | 1:47 | A sorted, ranked bar chart puts its claim at the ends, not the middle — the presenter reads the outliers straight off the axis ("the far end of this graph, you can see the Netherlands and Korea are in single figures") instead of a caption restating them. Sorting a comparison chart is itself the annotation; the extremes carry the sentence. |
| Alan Smith — [Why you should love statistics](https://www.ted.com/talks/alan_smith_why_you_should_love_statistics) (TED, 2016) | 7:56 | A proportion built as a count of repeating icons (Otto Neurath's isotype method) instead of a percentage removes the numeracy barrier a fraction imposes on part of the audience — "a great way of representing quantity without resorting to using terms like percentage." Choosing the unit of encoding is an inclusion decision, not a decorative one. |
| The New York Times Visual Investigations — [How the Police Killed Breonna Taylor](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lDaNU7yDnsc) (2020) | 4:20 | Every account is anchored inside one fixed spatial reconstruction before it is heard — "in this reconstruction, we hear the official testimonies given by the two officers nearest the door." Competing, contradictory testimony accumulates inside a shared frame instead of asking the reader to hold each version separately in their head. |
| Vox Atlas — [The conflict in Kashmir, explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cyayif_nla8) (2019) | 3:51 | The map's defining boundary is drawn on screen at the exact moment the narration reaches its creation — "the UN Security Council brokered a ceasefire in 1949, which established this line." Synchronizing a map's geography with the story's chronology reads as cause and effect, where showing the finished border up front and explaining it after would read as trivia. |
| Kurzgesagt — [The Coronavirus Explained & What You Should Do](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BtN-goy9VOY) (2020) | 5:20 | A capacity threshold drawn across a rising curve turns a shape claim into a consequence claim — "if the numbers get too large, health care systems become unable to handle it." The annotated ceiling, not the curve's steepness alone, is what makes the acceleration legible as a stake rather than just a trend. |
| David McCandless — [The beauty of data visualization](https://www.ted.com/talks/david_mccandless_the_beauty_of_data_visualization) (TED, 2010) | 1:25 | Placing a small quantity at true relative scale beside a much larger one in the same proportional-area space lets the reader feel the ratio instead of computing it — "780 billion a year... and this little pixel in the corner, three billion, that's their climate change fund." Scale is an argument only when both magnitudes share one encoded space. |
