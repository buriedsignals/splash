## The rule

The bar already points. A second hue says the same thing twice, and that is worth buying only where
the reader cannot hold the whole plate in one look.

## Four publications, two answers, two each

**One hue for both signs.** ONS gives its cyan to `Factories` (+300) and to `Warehouses` (−500)
alike, spending its only other tone on the *aggregate* row rather than on a sign. Our World in Data
gives all four bars `#7088B0` at 16.461 %, and the pixel route reads that palette as **monochrome**.

**One hue per sign.** Statista and Datawrapper split.

## And it corrects the doctrine

`references/types/diverging-bar.md` says *exactly two hues, one per sign*. **Two of the four
publications that draw this form do not.** The condition that separates them is visible in the
records: the one-hue charts have four bars and label every one of them; the two-hue charts have
eight and sixteen rows, and one of Statista's two signs is rare. So the rule is not a cap, it is a
threshold — and the threshold is whether the plate can be held in one look.

**Where two hues are spent, both desks pick warm against cool**, and neither picks red against
green: Statista's `Loss` orange `#FF7D3B` against a `Gain` teal, Datawrapper's `#D04F37` against
`#2B668C`. That is the one claim in the type sheet this corpus corroborates without qualification.

## What limits it

**A newsroom palette carrying one accent cannot spend two hues**, and the composer's rule is that a
recorded house palette wins over an imported practice. Where that happens the one-hue branch is not
a compromise, it is the branch two of the four publications took.

**And the measurement gap is stated.** Statista's `Gain` teal does not appear in its record's top-ten
chromatic entries at all — the gains are four short bars in sixteen rows. Its hue was looked at, not
measured, and no hex is quoted for it. The absence is itself that chart's subject.
