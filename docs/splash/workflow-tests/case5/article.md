# The Strait of Hormuz: the world's most important oil chokepoint

No dataset accompanies this piece — it is a geographic explainer.

Between Iran to the north and Oman's Musandam peninsula to the south lies a stretch
of water barely 33 kilometres wide at its narrowest. Through it, on an average day,
passes roughly a fifth of the world's petroleum — about 20 million barrels of oil and
a third of all seaborne liquefied natural gas.

Ships entering the Gulf funnel through two shipping lanes just three kilometres wide,
hugging Omani waters. They call at the great loading terminals of the Gulf: Saudi
Arabia's Ras Tanura, the largest offshore oil port in the world; Iran's Kharg Island;
the UAE's Fujairah bunkering hub, which sits just outside the strait; and Qatar's LNG
terminals at Ras Laffan.

When tension flares in the Gulf, it is this narrow gate — not the size of any one
country's reserves — that moves the oil price. There is no quick alternative: the few
bypass pipelines can carry only a fraction of the flow.

Source: US Energy Information Administration; geographic references from Natural Earth.
