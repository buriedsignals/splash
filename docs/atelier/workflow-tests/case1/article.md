# Solar's uneven map: Hungary now gets more than a quarter of its power from the sun

Solar power became a mainstream force in Europe's electricity mix in 2024 — but where the sun does the heavy lifting varies enormously from one country to the next. New country-level figures show a spread of more than 20 percentage points between Europe's solar leaders and its laggards.

Hungary tops the continent, generating 27.3 percent of its electricity from solar in 2024 — more than a quarter of the national total, and the highest share anywhere in Europe. It is followed by Spain at 21.9 percent and the Netherlands at 21.1 percent, two countries with very different climates but comparably aggressive solar build-outs. Greece rounds out the leading group at 19.8 percent.

A cluster of countries sits in the high teens. Lithuania reached 18.8 percent and Bulgaria 18.2 percent, both ahead of the far larger German market, where solar supplied 17.9 percent of electricity. Portugal (17.3 percent) and Italy (16.9 percent) were close behind, with Belgium at 15.6 percent.

Toward the lower end of the table, solar still plays a meaningful but smaller role. Austria drew 14.1 percent of its power from the sun and Denmark 13.4 percent — striking for a country better known for its wind turbines. Poland, a relative newcomer to solar, reached 11.3 percent, while Romania stood at 9.8 percent. Czechia trailed the group at 5.8 percent, the lowest share among these fifteen countries.

The gap between Hungary's 27.3 percent and Czechia's 5.8 percent — neighbours in central Europe — underlines how much policy, grid rules and investment, rather than latitude alone, shape a country's solar footprint. Sunny Spain leads the pack, but the cloudier Netherlands nearly matches it, and Denmark outpaces several southern peers.

Taken together, these national stories add up to a continental milestone: across the EU as a whole, solar supplied 11 percent of electricity in 2024, overtaking coal — at 10 percent — for the first time ever. EU solar generation reached 304 terawatt-hours, 22 percent more than a year earlier. The map of who is leading that shift, though, remains anything but uniform.
