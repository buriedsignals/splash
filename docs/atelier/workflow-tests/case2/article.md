# US home prices have cooled from their peak — but they are still far above 2020

The median price of a home sold in the United States has drifted down for a third straight year, offering weary buyers a modest reprieve after the fastest price surge in a generation. Yet the retreat has barely dented the pandemic-era run-up, and homes remain sharply more expensive than they were five years ago.

According to Census Bureau and HUD figures published through the Federal Reserve, the median sales price was $403,200 in the first quarter of 2026. That is down from the all-time peak of $442,600 reached in the fourth quarter of 2022 — a decline of about 9 percent from the top.

The climb that preceded the peak was extraordinary. At the start of 2020, before the pandemic reshaped the housing market, the median home sold for $322,600. Ultra-low mortgage rates and a scramble for space pushed that figure to $355,000 by early 2021 and to $433,100 by the first quarter of 2022 — a rise of more than a third in just two years.

Since the peak, higher borrowing costs have taken the heat out of the market. The median eased to $429,000 in early 2023 and to $420,800 in early 2024, before settling at $403,200 at the start of 2026. In other words, prices have given back only a fraction of the pandemic gains: today's median is still roughly a quarter above the $322,600 recorded at the start of 2020.

The longer view makes the shift starker still. As recently as January 2014, the median US home sold for just $275,200. From that low, prices have climbed by more than $128,000, or about 47 percent, in a little over a decade.

For buyers, the message is mixed. The worst of the price spike is behind them, and the median has now slipped for three consecutive years from its 2022 high. But with the typical home still costing well over $400,000 — far above pre-pandemic levels — affordability remains stretched, and the modest cooldown is unlikely to feel like relief to first-time buyers priced out during the boom.
